BASEBALL PLAIN-ENGLISH KEY — every stat on this page, in one glance:
PA = plate appearance — every time a batter steps to the plate (any outcome) OBP = how often a batter reaches base (hits + walks) contact % = how often the batter actually hits the ball (vs striking out) BB% = how often a batter takes a walk ISO = power — extra bases beyond singles HR / 1000 PA = home runs per 1,000 plate appearances (league ≈ 34–38; how often a batter's PA ends in a home run) Run differential = runs scored minus runs allowed (best single win predictor) AUC = how well a model separates winners from losers (1.0 = perfect, 0.5 = a coin flip) wOBA / wRC+ = overall hitting value, 100 = league average FIP = pitching quality (a pitcher's own doing; lower = better) K-BB% = strikeouts minus walks = how dominant a pitcher is WAR = wins above replacement, the famous player-value stat. Published free by FanGraphs (fWAR) & Baseball-Reference (bWAR), but the two formulas disagree, it is not returned by the MLB API, and it is not a league-published number — so this value panel shows WAR's reproducible inputs (wOBA, wRC+, FIP, K-BB%) instead. Note: the FA shopping list (this build) uses FanGraphs Steamer WAR.

Chapter 1

Contact rate is more persistent than on-base percentage.

Across four real season pairs, contact rate persists at r of about 0.8 to 0.9, while on-base percentage persists more weakly: r of about 0.55 to 0.59 in the first three pairs and 0.30 in the latest pair (2025 to 2026), pooled r of about 0.53.

Decision lens: Pay for the skill that produces on-base percentage, not for last year's on-base percentage.

Source: MLB Stats API player data, year over year.

Chapter 2

Run differential describes a season. It does not forecast one.

The same-season fit is strong, with R squared of about 0.55: a better run margin means more wins in that season. It is not a forward-looking prediction.

Decision lens: Use run differential to describe a season, never to promise the next one.

Source: MLB Stats API panel, 2011 to 2026, 509 team-seasons.

Ten fewer errors were associated with about 0.9 more wins per 162 games after accounting for club, season, offense and pitching (approximate 95% confidence interval: 0.5 to 1.3 wins).

Chapter 3

Money buys some wins. It does not buy the most wins per dollar.

The winning-cheap teams, Milwaukee, Tampa, and Cleveland, win about 95 games at a third to a quarter of big-market payroll.

Decision lens: Use efficient clubs as case studies, then test the fit for your own roster.

Source: MLB Stats API plus Spotrac payroll.

Chapter 4

The proposed cap changes the payroll gaps.

Seven teams are over the proposed cap and must cut, the Mets by $101.4M, while fifteen, including Cleveland at $96.7M to add, sit under the floor and are forced to spend.

Decision lens: Compare each payroll gap under the proposed rules before considering transactions.

Source: Spotrac payroll against the owners' proposed 2027 cap and floor.

Chapter 5

The floor roster captures most of the cap roster's talent.

Built entirely from the real 2027 free-agent class, the floor roster buys 48.9 WAR for $171.1M and the cap roster buys 54.8 WAR for $245.2M: about 89 percent of the talent for about 70 percent of the money. The builds are proven optimal, and across ±20% price error the floor holds 45.0 to 53.2 WAR. Delivered WAR runs lower and wider: the Monte Carlo over measured projection error and player availability puts the floor's median at 39.8 WAR (10th to 90th: 33.0 to 46.9) and the cap's at 44.9 (37.6 to 52.4).

Decision lens: Compare the marginal projected contribution with its additional cost and uncertainty.

Source: Spotrac free-agent class plus FanGraphs Steamer WAR, prior-contract price anchors; exact optimization, (Figure 25); delivered-WAR ranges, (data/uncertainty_scenarios_2027.json).

Chapter 6

Checked in public against the best benchmark we could get.

Our Marcel hitter projections match FanGraphs Steamer at OBP r of 0.889, with 201 hitters matched by player ID. The pitcher bias found in that check is disclosed on the panel.

Decision lens: Check both benchmark agreement and held-out error before relying on a projection.

What the data actually shows  (measured on 509 real team-seasons, 2011-2026)

1. Run differential drives wins, but only as a diagnostic. The same-season fit is strong (R² ≈ 0.55) — a better run margin means more wins. It is not a forward-looking prediction.

2. Raw hitting KPIs do not reliably forecast next-season wins. In-sample accuracy is weak (R² ≈ 0.14), and out-of-sample the model is no better than simply assuming the league average. Year-over-year team wins regress sharply toward the mean — consistent with established baseball research.

3. The defensible edge is at the player level, not the team level. A player's underlying skills — contact rate (pooled r = .84) and walk discipline (r = .66 to .77 in each of the four pairs) — persist year to year in ways that wins themselves do not. That persistence, paired with Marcel-style aging and health constraints, is what builds the 26-man roster shown here. We explicitly do not claim a forward-looking team-wins model: cross-year accuracy is roughly 9-13 wins, and we present that number honestly.

The posting clock

The window is drawn as the bottom panel of Figure 23 above; the facts: posted Nov 1, 2026 → negotiations open Nov 2 → CBA expires Dec 1 (the clock pauses during a lockout) → signing deadline Dec 16, 17:00 ET (45-day window).

Posting fee, paid to the NPB club on top of the contract: 20% of first $25M of MLB contract, 17.5% of next $25M, 15% above $50M, plus 15% on bonuses/options actually earned/vested.

Worked example — ILLUSTRATIVE, not a predicted price: on a hypothetical $60M contract the fee is 20% of $25M ($5.00M) + 17.5% of $25M ($4.375M) + 15% of $10M ($1.50M) = $10.875M. Under a hard cap, that fee is a real acquisition cost a GM must budget.

The verdict on our own pricing claim

The Hakes–Sauer salary tests examine whether the proposed skills are underpriced. The evidence below distinguishes supported findings from unresolved claims.

THE VERDICT — INCONCLUSIVE ON MISPRICING

INCONCLUSIVE on mispricing. The formal proportionality test (run-value benchmark from 480 team-seasons, ratio 0.94) cannot reject correct OBP-vs-SLG pricing in either salary sample (S, n = 60, p = 0.42; C, n = 82, p = 0.90) — and cannot reject large mispricing either: the samples lack power. What stands: the skills persist, and top-quartile contact hitters cost about half the median $/WAR of the bottom quartile on both salary bases — a descriptive contrast, not a price effect. The claim is persistent + cap-efficient + low-variance, not proven undervaluation. Design limit: active contracts, not newly-signed free agents.

Benchmark: team-season runs regression (runs per PA on OBP and SLG, season fixed effects) on data/curated/fact_team_season.parquet — a team-aggregate benchmark, labeled as such in the workpaper (docs/CARPE_SCORECARD.md, addendum). Salary samples: Spotrac basis n = 60 (24 of 30 teams, at the power floor) and Cot's LRP-2027 n = 82 — bases never mixed. Method and full numbers: src/bml_cycle48.py, data/hakes_sauer_verdict.json.

Takeaway: The optimizer finds the best roster within the supplied pool, prices and constraints. Mathematical optimality does not guarantee real-world feasibility.
