## HOWDY + THE FILM — Figure 1 (1:00)
Press play before you say a word. Sixteen seasons of payroll vs wins animate;
the cheap winners stay gold the whole time. *Then:* "The owners want a hard
cap. So the question isn't 'who spends the most' — that game is over. The
question is: **which team deploys a constrained budget better than the teams
it competes against?** And the answer is measurable."

## THE EDGE — Figure 3 (2:00)
Point at the bars. "Here's the single most important chart in the room.
We tracked real players year over year. What a player *does* that's a *skill*
— making contact, drawing walks — **carries. Across four real season pairs,
contact rate persists at r ≈ 0.8-0.9.** But the *output* owners are
tempted to buy — on-base percentage — persists more weakly: r ≈ 0.55-0.59
in the first three pairs, 0.30 in the latest pair (2025→2026), pooled
r ≈ 0.53 across 457 player-year pairs. It carries less than the skills do."

**The line:** "You don't pay for the OBP. You pay for the skill that produces
it. That's a measurable edge."

## WHY THIS ISN'T A GAMBLE — Figures 4-6 (1:30)
Fig 4 (run diff): "Better teams score more than they allow — that's real,
but it describes *this* season; it doesn't predict next."
Fig 5 (title-bar test): "Run differential alone flags a 90+ win club with
AUC ≈ 0.95 — both directions, both years. That's separation, not a forecast."
Fig 6 (payroll vs wins): "Money buys some wins. But the winning-cheap teams —
Milwaukee, Tampa, Cleveland — win ~95 at a third to a quarter the payroll.
**They're the ones who've discovered the edge we're showing you.**"

## HOW THE WINNERS ARE BUILT — Figures 7, 13-15 (2:30)

## THE CAP IS THE OPPORTUNITY — Figures 10 + 11 + 20 (1:30)
Fig 10 (cap reality): "Here's the kicker. Seven teams are over the cap and
MUST cut — the Mets $101M, the Dodgers $94M. And fifteen — **including
Cleveland (+$97M) and Tampa (+$76M)** — are under the floor and forced to
add. The cap makes the rich cut; the floor hands the disciplined winners
real money to deploy. **The system is now engineered to reward exactly the
teams that waste the least.**"
Fig 11 (forced sellers): "And it doesn't take a rebuild. Every over-cap
team closes its gap by shedding at most three big contracts — Toronto and
Atlanta need just one. The cap binds through a handful of deals. That is
arithmetic, not a prediction that anyone trades those players — but it
sizes the negotiation exactly."

Fig 20 (value metrics): "This is what we compute instead of faking WAR —
wOBA, wRC+, FIP. Real, reproducible."

## WHAT THE MONEY BUYS — Figures 16 + 22 (1:30)
"We built the actual free-agent rosters at each budget — real players, real
prices, and each roster is the proven optimum at its budget. The floor
roster — $171M — buys **49 wins above replacement**. The cap roster at

## HONESTY — what we will NOT claim (1:00)
"This is where we're different. We do **not** tell you we can predict team
wins — forecasting is genuinely weak (about 9-13 wins off), and saying
otherwise would be a lie that gets exposed. WAR is published free by FanGraphs
and Baseball-Reference — but the two formulas disagree and no MLB API returns
it — so we surface its real inputs instead. Our projections were
benchmarked against FanGraphs' Steamer in public (Fig 18): hitter agreement
r = .889. And we do **not** claim the market misprices these skills — we ran
the pricing test on ourselves (Fig 24): formally inconclusive at current
samples, so the claim stays persistence and cap-efficiency, not proven
undervaluation. What we *can* show, measured over four years: skills persist,
luck doesn't. **That's the edge you can act on.**"

## BACK-POCKET Q&A — Did you optimize the roster, or just fill it greedily? (Figure 25, Explorer only)
"Both — and we published the difference. The original build was greedy:
best WAR-per-dollar at each position, then upgrades while the budget
holds. In Cycle 49 we re-solved the identical problem — same 199
free agents, same Steamer WAR, same prior-contract prices, same quotas —
as an exact mixed-integer optimization, and the solver proved its optima.
In Cycle 52, with the owner's approval, the optimizer rosters became
Figure 16's headline; the greedy builds stay in Figure 25 as the
benchmark."
- **The gap:** Floor 42.0 → **48.9 WAR** (+6.9), Middle 45.7 → **52.8**
  (+7.1), Cap 52.3 → **54.8** (+2.5). The optimizer's floor roster beats
  the greedy's middle roster outright — construction quality is worth
  roughly a budget tier at the tight end.
- **Where it comes from:** mostly combinatorics — restricted to the
  greedy's own effective search space, the optimizer still finds +5.5 /
  +5.2 / +0.4. The rest is one rule the greedy never used: its own code
  says the DH can be any bat, but with two DH-mapped players in the pool
  its fallback never fired, so every greedy roster carries Dominic Smith
  (0.01 WAR) at DH. The optimizer plays Brandon Lowe (2.32 WAR) there.
- **The 15% rule:** the owners' proposed per-player maximum ($36.8M)
  binds nobody in this class at prior prices — the dearest anchor is
  $35.0M — and all three greedy rosters satisfy it. Enforced in the
  optimizer anyway; the check is on the record in Figure 25.
- **Why the greedy rosters are still on the page:** "They are the
  benchmark. The gap between a competent greedy build and the proven
  optimum is the evidence that construction quality is worth roughly a
  budget tier — so Figure 25 keeps both, labeled. The promotion itself
  was the owner's call (Cycle 52), not a silent edit."
Source for every line: data/optimizer_comparison_2027.json (Cycle 49,
src/bml_cycle49.py; solver scipy/HiGHS MILP, gap 0 at all three budgets).

## BACK-POCKET Q&A — What about Japan? (Figure 23, Explorer only)
"The three best NPB players were posted Nov 1 — negotiations open Nov 2,
the window closes Dec 16 at 5pm ET, and the clock pauses if the Dec 1
lockout begins. This is the one part of the 2027 market that resolves
while we're in the room."
- **Teruaki Sato (Hanshin, 3B/OF):** .306, 39 HR, 106 RBI, .992 OPS — a
  Triple Crown, and #1 among the Central League's 19 qualified bats in
  all three categories — but a 26.7% strikeout rate against a 16.0%
  qualified median. "That's the exception you price, not the fit you
  assume. His walk rate rising 7.3% → 9.5% → 11.1% is the counterweight."
- **Hiromi Itoh (Nippon-Ham, RHP):** 3.18 ERA, 172.2 IP, 4.7% walk rate
  (3.6% in 2025, 5.5% in 2024) — three straight seasons of 172+ innings,
  and the #2 walk rate among the Pacific League's 11 ERA qualifiers even
  in a year his ERA ranked only 7th. "That is exactly the low-walk,
  available arm this model rewards — the fit is the walks, not the ERA."
- **Kaima Taira (Seibu, RHP):** PL-best 1.36 ERA — #1 of 11 qualifiers —
  in his first year as a starter, one season after a PL-best 31 saves at
  a 1.71 ERA. "The question is workload: 146 innings is his career high
  by about 90 — a +93.8 IP jump in one year."
- **The fee is real money:** 20% of the first $25M, 17.5% of the next $25M,
  15% above $50M — on top of the contract. The curve in Figure 23:
  $25M → $5.0M, $50M → $9.375M, $60M → $10.875M, $100M → $16.875M.
  "Under a hard cap, budget the fee, not just the player."
- **The caution:** "We claim no NPB-to-MLB translation factor anywhere.
  Tatsuya Imai was projected at 6 years / $150M last winter, signed 3
  years / $54M with Houston, then posted a 4.93 ERA and was moved to the
  bullpen. NPB dominance is evidence, not a projection."
Source for every line: NPB official BIS (BIS is canonical over wire
roundings — Sato .306 not .308, Itoh 34 walks not 32); posting facts via
AP / MLB Trade Rumors / Sportsnet.
